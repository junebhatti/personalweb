Essays & Arguments
Two of today’s essays catch the same error from opposite ends. Steve Huynh shows how much we overrate other people’s attention to us; Richard Chappell shows how readily we underrate their reasons, treating disagreement as proof of bad faith. Both shrink what you’ll do: walk strangely, say the true thing, argue with someone you love. Geoffrey Litt adds that the frictions of dealing with people are often where connection lives.
 
Today’s question
If almost no one is watching, whose judgment are you still performing for?
ESSAY  Good Thoughts · Richard Y. Chappell
Hostility Requires a Warrant ↗
A philosopher argues that hostility toward someone is justified only by ill will or unreasonableness, never by policy disagreement alone. Because blame tracks what a person could know, he asks critics to check why the other side thinks it is being reasonable before assuming it isn’t. The hook: a commenter suggests reasonableness includes what you should have found out, which makes many fights about homework, not values. Feeds your note: disagreement isn’t zero-sum.
ESSAY  A Life Engineered · Steve Huynh
Nobody’s Watching ↗
A longtime Amazon engineer recalls being mortified by a crinkling chip bag in a tense negotiation; no one noticed. The spotlight effect cuts both ways: the stares you dread mostly aren’t there, and neither is the attention you assume your good work gets. His fix is to worry less about being seen and report plainly, and often, what you’ve actually done. Feeds your note: the stares while walking backward.
ESSAY  geoffreylitt.com · Geoffrey Litt
AI as teleportation ↗
Litt imagines cheap teleportation in 2035: commutes vanish, and so do walks and chance encounters. Borrowing Albert Borgmann’s wood stove, which heated the house and also gathered the family, he asks which frictions AI removes that were secretly doing social work (asking a friend, not a model, to explain something), and keeps some on purpose. Feeds your note: is convenience killing connection.

My AI Adoption Journey ↗
The creator of Terraform and Ghostty lays out six stages from skeptic to always-on agents, each a copyable habit. Redo your own past work with an agent to learn its limits, split planning from execution, give it a way to verify itself, and turn every repeated mistake into a line in AGENTS.md. The twist: start research runs before you stop for the day and read the reports in the morning.
