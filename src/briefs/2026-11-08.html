Tighter blood pressure control cut cardiac events after stroke in a large trial
In a New England Journal of Medicine trial of 4,368 stroke or TIA survivors in Brazil, a systolic target under 120 mm Hg cut major cardiovascular events to 10.9%, against 13.5% with a target under 140, over a median 3.9 years.
WHY  It worked by holding pressure lower for years (mean 122.0 vs 135.2 mm Hg), at a cost: renal events rose to 3.9% from 2.2%. Next: whether an open-label result from one country changes guidelines.

AI-designed bird flu vaccine fully protected animals across H5 variants
A Cambridge-led team reported in Cell Reports Medicine that the vaccine fully protected mice and ferrets against genetically distinct bird flu variants, while only half the animals given an existing stockpiled vaccine survived.
WHY  AI combined global surveillance sequences into synthetic antigens carrying features shared across the group, instead of targeting one strain. So far this is animal data only; human trials are the next step.

Essays & Arguments
Today’s three pieces share a question: what goes missing when something gets easier? Lara Ferris is tempted by an assistant to run her schedule, then remembers a bus driver letting her ride free. Brett Wick argues taste grows through struggle that tools may now absorb, yet notes that AlphaZero widened what grandmasters thought was good. And in 1909 Chesterton defended the morning wasted for no reason at all.
 
Today’s question
What in your day would you refuse to hand to an agent, even if it did it better?
ESSAY  Lines of Desire · Lara Ferris
Escaping the automation ↗
Ferris argues that automation removes more than admin: it removes the small checks and delays that build judgment. She grants the appeal of an assistant for her overloaded calendar, then sets it beside the music and film she found through listings and radio. She never settles where tedious admin ends and necessary friction begins. It pushes your note on agents trading autonomy for mental freedom.
ESSAY  Navigating Proof Space · Brett Wick
What a Chess Engine Knows About Taste ↗
A working mathematician argues that taste is a learned model for predicting which problems will prove fruitful, and that part of it can be taught. His method comes from wine and art: compare strong work with weak, say why, get corrected. AlphaZero revived sacrifices humans had discarded, so a tool enlarged judgment instead of replacing it. What he says can’t be taught is the nerve to decline the fashionable direction. It extends your note on outsourcing taste.
CLASSIC  Quotidiana · G. K. Chesterton
On Lying in Bed ↗
Chesterton begins by wanting to paint his ceiling, the one blank surface in a cluttered house, and ends up defending the horizontal morning. His target is a society that moralizes small habits like early rising while letting large virtues slide. About five minutes long, it has lasted because the joke turns out to be meant. It connects to your note on the optimization crisis.

WORKFLOW  geoffreylitt.com
Code like a surgeon ↗
Litt rejects the idea that AI turns you into a manager: he does the core work and has agents handle the support tasks. He hands off codebase guides, throwaway spikes, well-specified bugs and docs, and keeps design prototyping close. Primary work needs tight supervision and secondary work can run long unattended, and blending the two is where it goes wrong. The open question: how do beginners learn if grunt work disappears?

AI PAPERS  simonwillison.net
Qwen 3.8 27B on my laptop can interpret model weights, too ↗
A recent paper claimed a frontier model can infer a network’s training task from raw weights alone. Willison tried it on a 17GB local model, which named Breakout, Pong and Pac-Man-style games in runs near an hour each and failed on Freeway. A patient human could probably do the same, he notes; the surprise is what now runs locally.
